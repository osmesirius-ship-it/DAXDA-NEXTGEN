# GIRL, UNTROUBLED
## Chapter 3 — The House That Looked Like Enough

My grandmother’s home in Paradise Valley was the first place in a long time where my life had a shape again.

We lived at Bonaventure Mobile Home Park, near Seventh Street and Utopia. The neighborhood was quiet. The kids were nice. There were scooters and friends to play with outside. I did not have to listen for a door bursting open. I did not have to calculate the next ten seconds before breathing.

My room was clean. There was an artificial tree with lights and a large black-metal canopy bed with sheer white fabric draped over it. I had a vanity and Polly Pockets. In the mornings, I watched *Max and Ruby* and *Rugrats*. Later, *The Young and the Restless* came on, and sometimes I fell asleep beside my grandmother for an afternoon nap.

There were trips to Denny’s. My grandfather came home from work. We had family barbecues twice a month, and my grandfather grilled steaks once a month. Ordinary things—the kind a child who has always had them never thinks to inventory.

I had not always had them.

The peace was not complete. My grandmother had retired early to help raise me, and later my baby sister too. Even as a child, I knew taking care of us was not the life she had planned. I could feel the resentment sitting right beside the care.

Those feelings could live in the same room. I could be grateful and still feel unwanted. I could be safe and still feel guilty for needing safety.

My mother remained the variable no routine could account for.

She moved in and out of my life. My grandmother rooted for her, sometimes to a fault, as if enough chances might eventually turn her into the mother we needed. Meanwhile, I was the child sitting in a classroom, physically present and mentally elsewhere, trying to solve a problem no child can solve: where was my mother, and when would she come back?

Then my mother had another baby.

### The birthday I didn't get to have

I was eight when my little sister was born. I did not know my mother was pregnant.

My eighth birthday party was planned for a Saturday. My grandmother had invited friends from school. The night before, I was sleeping over at a friend’s house when her mother told me I would be staying another night because my mother was having a baby.

I did not understand. My mother had once asked me what I would do if she had a baby, but she had never told me she was pregnant. I did not know a new person was arriving, or that her arrival meant my day was gone.

I called my grandmother and cried. I cried hard. I was supposed to have a birthday party. Instead, I was finding out from somebody else’s mother that my life was being rearranged again.

I missed my eighth birthday.

My mother could not take the baby home from the hospital because of her drug use, so my grandmother brought her home.

I remember looking at my sister in her bassinet. She drooled. She cried. I looked at her and thought, *Ew. Fucking ew.*

I did not want to take care of her. I had barely begun to get a piece of childhood back, and suddenly there was someone smaller who needed everything.

Whenever she woke up, I was supposed to wake my grandmother. Sometimes waking her was hard, so I learned to feed the baby myself. Diapers made me gag—I remember the physical urge to throw up, and the fact that the diaper still had to be changed.

As she grew, the list grew with her. Jars of baby food. Rice pudding. After school, I made french fries and rice with cilantro and onions. She liked Hershey’s Kisses and Happy Meals. She kept a bottle for years, along with a pacifier and a blanket. Even then, it looked to me like the adults were pacifying her instead of fixing what was broken around her.

I was a child being turned into an authority figure for another child. I was learning how to keep my sister settled while still needing someone to care for me.

### Pink silk, purple silk

My mother had been in a car accident, and around the time I turned nine, a settlement came through—roughly four hundred thousand dollars. She had been living with my grandmother after the accident. With the money, she got an apartment, and my grandmother returned my sister and me to her care.

When I heard I was moving back in with my mother, I felt a pit in my stomach.

I also felt hope.

Maybe this time she had it together. Maybe the money meant stability. Maybe it would last.

I still remember going to Ashley Furniture with her. She was electric with excitement—generous, proud, eager to build a home. The store felt enormous. Crossing the parking lot, she gripped my hand way too hard, the way she always did when we crossed roads. I used to joke that squeezing my hand like that would not stop a car from hitting us.

Inside, she let me choose my own bedroom set.

It was white, with pink silk on one side and purple silk on the other. I ran around testing beds, picturing what my room would look like. Afterward, we went to Walmart and picked out bedding, a rug, a little television, and a Strawberry Shortcake CD player.

Those were not abstract promises. They were real objects for a room that belonged to me. My mother was spending money on me, asking what I wanted, letting me choose.

For a little while, I felt loved.

I do not want to erase the good afternoons just because they did not survive. The furniture was real. Choosing it was real. So was the quiet fear underneath it that all of it could vanish.

It did. In six or seven months, the money was gone.

That Christmas, I woke up and found my mother on the couch, messed up, with the presents only halfway wrapped. I asked if Santa was not coming.

She told me to go back to bed.

I went back to sleep. When I woke up again, I pretended to be excited.

I knew what I had seen. I was learning how to perform happiness around disappointment—how to live inside something that looked good from the outside and shook underneath.

### The field trip

In third grade, my class took a field trip to Arizona State University. My mother came as a chaperone.

At lunchtime, everyone else had sack lunches, but my mother decided to get me Burger King from the food court—and then ordered burgers for the entire class. That was her kind of gesture: loud, generous, bigger than ordinary.

The order took longer than lunch.

My mother did not come back.

I do not remember seeing the burgers or what happened when we got home. I remember the school bus. I remember crying the entire ride back in front of my whole class.

It was not about lunch. My mother had come as a chaperone—one of the adults responsible for making sure we were safe—and she had disappeared again. There was nowhere private to put that feeling on a school bus. I just sat there and cried.

### Castles N' Coasters

Later that year, for my ninth birthday, we went to Castles N’ Coasters. After missing my eighth birthday party, I was thrilled to finally have a real one. I was with my brothers, having a good time.

Then my mother said she had to go somewhere and would be right back.

The pit in my stomach returned instantly. I knew that feeling before I had words for it—the sound of a promise you cannot trust.

Near closing time, I was running to catch one last line before the park shut down. I ran straight into a pole.

My head throbbed. I started crying. I could not find my brothers. I wanted my mother.

Then, out of nowhere, she appeared—asking what had happened to my head.

She had been gone, and suddenly she was there, responding to the emergency that happened while she was absent. That was the difference I was beginning to feel without being able to name it: a mother who showed up after you hit the pole, and a mother who was there to keep you from running alone in the dark.

That was the second slip.

### Nine years old

One afternoon, walking home from school, I saw my mother walking down the street without the baby. I asked where my sister was.

She told me the baby was at the apartment and told me to hurry home. She said she would be right back.

I ran. My sister was asleep.

I got home around three-thirty. When my sister woke up crying, I made a bottle, fed her, changed her diaper, and tried to settle her when she kept crying.

I was nine years old.

The light faded. Afternoon turned into evening. Evening turned into night. I was scared after dark, holding my baby sister until we curled up together and fell asleep.

My mother did not come back until two-thirty in the morning.

She walked in with a story about nearly being shot in the head.

There was always a story. Always an emergency that explained why she was gone.

I looked at her and said, “Whatever, sure, Mom.”

That was the third slip. First the field trip. Then my birthday. Now leaving a nine-year-old with a baby until two-thirty in the morning. I could not leave my sister. I could not go looking for my mother.

I could only do what was in front of me.

Feed her. Change her. Hold her. Wait.

### Under the table

Things got worse when my sister’s father got out of jail. He had gone to jail for beating my mother, and when he came back, the violence came back with him.

One night, a fight broke out that felt endless. He was beating my mother in the face and choking her. She was punching him and holding a knife, and I thought she was going to stab him.

My sister and I hid underneath the kitchen table.

I knew I needed to call the police, so I dialed—but I was too little to know our address. I hung up, terrified, while the screaming went on around us. The fight lasted long enough for the police to triangulate the call and arrive at the door.

When they banged on it, the fight stopped.

My mother opened the door and tried to act like nothing was wrong. She told them everything was fine.

Underneath the table, my sister and I were still screaming and crying.

The police forced their way in. They saw her face. They arrested my sister’s father. Then they asked my mother for her name and Social Security number, and she gave them my Aunt Alice’s—because she had a warrant and was terrified of going to jail herself.

She called my grandmother.

My grandmother came right away and took all of us back to her house.

I was excited. I was going home—back to my room, my grandparents, my friends, my school, and a house where I did not have to keep another child alive under a kitchen table.

### The last time I waited

By then, the four hundred thousand dollars was gone. The apartment, the pink and purple silk, the feeling of starting over—all of it had already passed.

Back at my grandmother’s house, my mother stayed for a while. Every morning before school, I asked her the same question: “Are you going to be here when I get home?”

She always said yes. Most days, she was.

I kept asking anyway. I needed to hear the promise out loud.

Then one night, the clock kept moving.

Nine o’clock. Ten. Eleven.

I had school in the morning. My mother had not come back, and I knew she was not coming.

I did not see her for two years after that.

For a long time, I carried the question of her everywhere, even into the classroom. As I got older, longing started turning into anger. My logic was simple then: when you love something, you fight to protect it. If you do not fight, you must not love it.

I know now it is not that simple. I believe my mother loved me to the edge of her capacity—it just was not a capacity that could keep a child safe. Love and consistency are not the same thing. Knowing that does not erase the girl who waited at the window or cried on the school bus. It only lets me hold both truths at once.

By the end of third grade, something inside me quietly shifted. Months had passed without an explanation, and the waiting stopped taking up all the air in my head.

I started focusing again. Fourth grade was a pretty good year.

I was not healed. I did not have my answers. I had not stopped loving my mother or being angry with her.

But for years, I had been listening for the sound of someone returning.

Now, for a little while, I could listen to something else.
